The Framework

Five Dimensions
of a Good Job

Each dimension captures a distinct aspect of job quality. The first three measure the job itself. The fourth measures the worker's power relative to it. The fifth measures whether structural barriers shape access and experience.

1
Economic Security
What the Job Provides

Wages adequate to living costs, stability of income and hours, protection against arbitrary dismissal, and access to non-wage benefits. Measures the resources the job delivers while you are in it.

Key Indicators
Wages relative to local living costs
Hours stability and predictability
Employer-provided benefits (healthcare, leave)
Protection against unfair dismissal
Income growth over time
AI impact: algorithmic scheduling can destabilize hours; AI-based performance monitoring can increase dismissal risk for otherwise adequate workers.
2
Work Quality
How the Work Feels

Autonomy and discretion in daily tasks, meaningfulness of contribution, scheduling control, freedom from intensive surveillance or coercive management, and time for life outside work.

Key Indicators
Task autonomy and discretion
Sense of meaning and contribution
Intensity of monitoring / surveillance
Working hours and rest time
Psychological safety at work
AI impact: the most immediate effect on this dimension — AI can augment skill and meaning, or hollow out discretion and replace it with algorithmic instruction.
3
Development & Progression
Where the Job Can Take You

Access to genuine skill development, clarity and realism of career pathways, actual (not nominal) mobility across occupational tiers. Distinct from credential accumulation — focused on whether capabilities and options genuinely grow.

Key Indicators
On-the-job learning and skill growth
Access to training and retraining
Realistic pathways to advancement
Wage growth over career
Cross-occupational mobility
AI impact: risk of deskilling if AI scaffolds tasks workers would otherwise master; opportunity if AI frees time for higher-order learning.
4
Labor Market Freedom
Your Power to Say No

The degree to which a worker can exit a bad job without catastrophic consequences. Measured by what exists independently of this job: social protection adequacy, portability of benefits, availability of alternatives, and whether basic needs are decoupled from employment.

Key Indicators
Unemployment insurance adequacy and duration
Portability of healthcare and pensions
Quality and coverage of universal basic services
Alternative job availability in local market
Retrenchment benefit upon dismissal
AI impact: as AI displaces occupations, exit power matters more — workers in at-risk roles with weak outside options face the most coercive conditions.
5
Structural Fairness
Who Gets Good Jobs

Whether actual job quality outcomes differ by race, gender, class, disability, or immigration status — and why. Not just whether discrimination is prohibited, but whether structural barriers produce systematically unequal experiences of all four other dimensions.

Key Indicators
Wage gaps by gender, race, class
Differential AI exposure by demographic group
Access to progression by background
Migrant and gig worker inclusion
Anti-discrimination enforcement effectiveness
AI impact: AI systems trained on historical data may encode and amplify existing inequalities in hiring, promotion, and task assignment.
Key Distinction

The Job vs.
The Worker's Position

Most job quality frameworks measure attributes of the job: what it pays, how safe it is, whether it offers training. This framework adds a structural layer — measuring the worker's position relative to the job, not just the job itself.

Dimensions 1–3 characterize the job while you are in it. Dimension 4 characterizes your power at the boundary — what happens if you leave, or are pushed out. Dimension 5 asks whether these experiences are distributed fairly across the workforce.

This matters because the same job can be experienced as freely chosen by one worker and coercive by another, depending entirely on their outside options. A $3,000/month job in a city with affordable housing, portable healthcare, and strong unemployment insurance is a different kind of freedom than the same job in a city where leaving means losing your home and healthcare simultaneously.

"Work is only truly voluntary when the worker has a genuinely livable alternative to doing it."
Case Studies

Applying the Framework

Six cases across countries and job types — illustrating how the five dimensions reveal different strengths and failures that wage data alone obscures.

Singapore · Healthcare · Public Sector
Registered Nurse,
Singapore Public Hospital
Overall Assessment
Mixed — Security without Freedom
Economic Security Strong
Work Quality Moderate
Development Strong
Labor Market Freedom Moderate
Structural Fairness Mixed
Framework Analysis

Singapore's public hospital nurses score well on economic security — wages have risen substantially, CPF is fully contributed, and job security is high in a sector with structural labor shortage. Career ladders are formally defined and accessible. The weakness is work quality: nursing ratios are stretched, shift work is intense, and AI monitoring tools are entering clinical environments. Labor market freedom is moderate — CPF makes exit costly in the short term, and housing affordability constrains geographic mobility. Structural fairness is mixed: the sector is female-dominated at the base but male-dominated in senior clinical roles, and migrant nurses face different conditions and limited pathways to citizenship.

Strengths
Strong CPF contributions and employer benefits. Clear career progression through clinician ladder. High job security in shortage occupation. Government investment in AI-assisted diagnostics creating some augmentation opportunities.
Weaknesses
Shift intensity and nurse-patient ratios limit work quality. AI monitoring of clinical performance creates stress without proportionate autonomy gain. Migrant nurses face significant structural disadvantages. Limited flexibility for caregiving responsibilities.
AI Trajectory
AI in diagnostics and medication management could free nurses for relational care — but only if job redesign is worker-centered. Risk is that AI productivity gains are absorbed into higher caseloads rather than improved work quality.

The 2024 Platform Workers Act improved CPF contributions and work injury compensation — a meaningful step in Dimension 1. But income remains volatile, algorithmic pricing and order assignment are opaque and largely uncontestable, and the scheduler's logic prioritizes platform efficiency over rider welfare. Work quality is mixed: genuine scheduling autonomy exists, but is undercut by economic pressure to accept all orders. Development is near-zero — the job builds no transferable skills or credentials. Labor market freedom is low not because of golden handcuffs but because the skills gap and the absence of CPF savings history make transition to formal employment difficult. The rider population skews toward minority communities with fewer alternatives, raising structural fairness concerns.

Strengths
Genuine scheduling flexibility. Post-Platform Workers Act: CPF contributions now mandatory, work injury compensation extended. Low barrier to entry provides income for those with limited formal credentials.
Weaknesses
Algorithmic management with no meaningful contestation rights. Income volatility. No skill development or career pathway. Health and safety risks borne entirely by the worker. Deactivation possible without transparent process.
AI Trajectory
AI route optimization and surge pricing already central to working conditions. Longer term, autonomous delivery threatens the role entirely — with no retraining pathway built into the employment relationship. The Platform Workers Act creates a regulatory moment for algorithmic transparency requirements.
United States · Technology · Private Sector
Software Engineer,
US Tech Company (Mid-size)
Overall Assessment
Mixed — High Reward, Low Freedom
Economic Security Strong
Work Quality Strong
Development Strong
Labor Market Freedom Weak
Structural Fairness Moderate
Framework Analysis

The US software engineer case reveals the framework's most important insight: high scores on Dimensions 1–3 can coexist with very low labor market freedom. Wages are high, work is intellectually engaging, and skills development is rapid. But Dimension 4 is structurally weak: US healthcare is tied to employment, meaning job loss triggers simultaneous income loss and healthcare loss. There is no meaningful unemployment insurance for higher earners. The 2023–2024 tech layoffs exposed how precarious this position was — workers with $200k salaries had 90 days of COBRA payments as their only safety net. Structural fairness is moderate: gender and racial gaps persist in advancement, but the sector is formally meritocratic in hiring.

Strengths
High wages and strong compensation packages. Significant intellectual autonomy and interesting problems. Rapid skill development. Generally strong employment market historically, though cooling. Remote work flexibility common post-pandemic.
Weaknesses
Healthcare tied to employment — exit is existentially risky for workers with health needs. Inadequate unemployment insurance relative to income. At-will employment means job security is dependent on employer goodwill. AI coding tools raising productivity expectations significantly.
AI Trajectory
AI coding assistants already reshaping the role — raising output expectations and potentially compressing junior roles. The long-term risk is that AI narrows the programmer skill funnel, creating a barbell between high-level architects and AI-prompt specialists with less in between. This affects Dimension 3 most acutely.
Denmark · Manufacturing · Union Sector
Manufacturing Technician,
Denmark (Unionized)
Overall Assessment
Strong — The Benchmark Case
Economic Security Strong
Work Quality Strong
Development Strong
Labor Market Freedom Very Strong
Structural Fairness Strong
Framework Analysis

Denmark's flexicurity model is the clearest real-world approximation of what high labor market freedom looks like institutionally. Unemployment insurance replaces up to 90% of previous wages for two years. Healthcare and childcare are universal and decoupled from employment. This means a Danish manufacturing worker can genuinely evaluate a job on its merits — the cost of leaving is real but not catastrophic. Collective bargaining produces strong wages and working conditions. The result is that even a relatively routine manufacturing job scores highly across all five dimensions — not because the job itself is extraordinary, but because the institutional context transforms the worker's position in relation to it. The main limitation is structural fairness: gender gaps persist in manufacturing, and immigrant workers face different conditions in some sectors.

Strengths
Flexicurity model: easy hiring and firing balanced by generous unemployment insurance. Universal healthcare and childcare fully decouple basic needs from employment. Strong union negotiation produces good wages and working conditions. Active labor market policy funds retraining generously.
Weaknesses
High tax burden funds the system — a political constraint on model expansion. Some routine manufacturing tasks face automation risk. Immigrant integration into the high-trust labor relations model is uneven. Benefits access can be complex for non-citizens.
AI Trajectory
Denmark's strong retraining infrastructure means AI displacement is more manageable than in comparable economies. The flexicurity model was explicitly designed for structural economic transitions — AI is the latest version of that challenge. The key question is whether retraining programs keep pace with the speed of AI-driven occupational change.

Part-Time Retail Worker,
United Kingdom (Large Chain)
Overall Assessment
Weak — Precarity on All Dimensions
Economic Security Weak
Work Quality Weak

Framework Analysis

The UK part-time retail worker illustrates how precarity compounds across dimensions. Zero-hours or low-hours contracts mean income is volatile and planning is impossible. AI-driven scheduling (now common in large UK retail chains) optimizes for store efficiency, creating unpredictable shift patterns that make childcare, second jobs, and life planning structurally difficult. Autonomy on the floor is minimal; monitoring is intensive. Development is near-zero for most workers. The one relative strength is Dimension 4 — the UK's NHS provides healthcare independent of employment, and the benefits system (though depleted by austerity) provides some floor. Structural fairness is poor: the part-time retail workforce skews heavily female and minority, and progression to management is limited by the same scheduling instability that defines the role.

Strengths
NHS provides healthcare independent of employment — meaningful for Dimension 4. National Living Wage sets a floor. Some worker protections stronger than US equivalents. Low barrier to entry provides accessible income.
Weaknesses
AI scheduling creates severe hours instability. Intensive electronic monitoring of pace and breaks. Near-zero training investment. Benefits system erosion reduces the safety net. Concentrated in communities with fewer alternatives, limiting real exit power.
AI Trajectory
AI self-checkouts and inventory management are already reducing headcount in large retail. AI scheduling is intensifying the hours-instability problem. The role faces both displacement risk and quality degradation simultaneously — the worst combination in the framework.
Singapore · Essential Services · Lower-Wage
Cleaning Supervisor,
Singapore (PWM Sector)
Overall Assessment
Improving — PWM Making a Difference
Economic Security Moderate
Work Quality Weak
Development Moderate
Labor Market Freedom Moderate
Structural Fairness Mixed
Framework Analysis

Singapore's Progressive Wage Model has materially improved Dimension 1 for cleaning supervisors — wages have risen from around $1,000 to over $2,500 for experienced supervisors, with mandatory progression tied to skills training. This is a policy success. But the framework reveals what PWM doesn't address: work quality remains low, with intensive physical demands, chemical exposure, and increasing deployment of tracking technology to monitor cleaning rounds. Labor market freedom is moderate — CPF provides a partial buffer, but the housing cost structure means low-wage workers have limited genuine exit options. Structural fairness is mixed: the sector is dominated by older workers and minority communities, and the wage gains, while real, leave workers still significantly below Singapore's Minimum Income Standard.

Strengths
PWM has delivered real wage growth — nearly 6% real income increase 2019–2024 for lower-wage workers. Mandatory CPF contributions provide partial social protection floor. Sector-level training pathways formalized. Job security relatively high given structural labor shortage.
Weaknesses
Physical intensity and health risks remain high. GPS and sensor-based monitoring of cleaning rounds increasing surveillance without corresponding autonomy. Wages still below MIS for older single workers. Migrant workers in adjacent roles face significantly worse conditions with no PWM coverage.
AI Trajectory
Cleaning robots are already deployed in Singapore's malls, hospitals, and public spaces. The PWM is explicitly tied to technology adoption — workers are expected to upskill to manage automated equipment. The risk is that automation reduces headcount faster than the workforce can transition, and that productivity gains flow to cleaning contractors and building owners rather than remaining workers.

Synthesis · 2026

What
research
says about
AI impact

Five bodies of literature. Seven frameworks. Three points of genuine consensus. And the critical gaps that our own archetype model must fill.

Multiple research traditions have developed frameworks for classifying how AI transforms jobs — not just whether. They converge on some patterns and diverge sharply on others.

Task-based economics
Acemoglu & Restrepo, Autor, Eloundou et al. — displacement, reinstatement, and the task transformation lens
Work organisation & management
Kellogg, Valentine & Christin, Eurofound, Wood et al. — algorithmic control and its dimensions
Normative frameworks
ILO, OECD, IFOW, WEF — how institutions translate AI exposure into job quality outcomes
Empirical workplace studies
Brynjolfsson et al., Eurofound EWCS, CIPD — what actually happens when AI is deployed
Existing frameworks

How the literature classifies AI job transformation

Each research tradition carves the problem differently. None covers all five dimensions of the Good Jobs Framework equally. Understanding where each tradition is strong — and where it is silent — tells us what any synthesis must supply.

Task displacement & reinstatement
2003–2024
Autor, Levy & Murnane · Acemoglu & Restrepo · Eloundou et al.
The dominant academic framework. AI automates tasks (displacement effect), raises productivity (productivity effect), and in principle creates new tasks (reinstatement effect). Occupations transform when their task bundle shifts, not when the job title disappears.
Task automation — routine tasks removed from human scope
Task augmentation — AI tools raise output on retained tasks
Task reinstatement — AI creates entirely new task types
"So-so automation" — displacement without sufficient reinstatement (Acemoglu's critique)
Covers economic security and development well. Largely silent on work quality, autonomy, and structural fairness.
Algorithmic management: the 6 Rs
2020
Kellogg, Valentine & Christin · Academy of Management Annals
Six mechanisms through which algorithms direct and discipline workers: Restricting (limiting options), Recommending (nudging behaviour), Recording (data collection), Rating (scoring performance), Replacing (substituting for human decisions), Rewarding (incentive allocation). These mechanisms combine into distinct archetypes of algorithmic control.
High-restriction jobs — AI dictates pace, sequence, and method
Surveillance-dominant jobs — recording and rating without restriction
Recommender-augmented jobs — AI as advisor, not director
Platform replacement — human judgment replaced by automated decision
The richest framework for work quality (dim 2). Less useful for wages, development, or structural fairness.
Generative AI at work — productivity & skill compression
2025
Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond · Quarterly Journal of Economics
The most rigorous causal workplace study to date. AI assistance raises average productivity 15% but compresses within-occupation skill premiums: novice workers gain ~30%, top performers gain little or become slightly less productive. AI transfers tacit knowledge from expert to novice, changing who within an occupation captures value.
Expert leverage — AI multiplies the highest performers' reach
Expertise levelling — novices catch up; expertise premium compresses
Knowledge transfer — AI externalises tacit expert knowledge; may deskill next cohort
Strong on wages within-occupation and short-run development. Silent on autonomy, freedom, and structural dimensions. Customer-service context may not generalise broadly.
AI and job quality: augmentation vs. intensification
2023–2025
Eurofound EWCS 2024 · OECD AI-WIPS · IFOW / Pissarides Review
Empirical survey evidence from Europe distinguishes two dominant patterns: augmentation (AI removes tasks, freeing workers for higher-value work) and intensification (AI adds tasks and raises pace, degrading work quality). Both coexist and which predominates depends on job design and institutional choices, not AI capability alone.
Augmented roles — AI removes low-value tasks; human output and satisfaction rise
Intensified roles — AI adds tasks and raises pace expectations; workers experience degradation
Redesigned roles — deliberate job redesign captures AI gains for worker benefit
EWCS covers work quality and development well; EU context may not apply to Singapore's weaker worker-voice institutions.
Fissured workplace × AI surveillance
2014–present
Weil · Schwartz ("Watchful AI") · Wood et al.
AI amplifies the fissured workplace dynamic: lead firms shed direct employment while retaining algorithmic control over subcontracted, franchised, and platform workers. AI tools extend algorithmic management to non-employees, intensifying surveillance without employment protections. This creates a compounded structural archetype for the most vulnerable workers.
Fissured-and-watched — non-employee, algorithmically managed, no recourse
Platform-controlled — algorithmic pricing, rating, and deactivation
Monitored subcontractor — employee in name, managed algorithmically at distance
Best framework for labor market freedom (dim 4) and structural fairness (dim 5). Explicitly links AI to the institutional context that determines worker power.
ILO: augmentation vs. automation of tasks
2023–2025
Gmyrek, Berg & Bescond · ILO Working Papers 96 & 140
The ILO explicitly separates exposure from displacement and distinguishes automation-exposed tasks (AI can substitute) from augmentation-exposed tasks (AI assists). This produces a two-by-two that maps onto four patterns of how occupations are affected. Importantly, the ILO finds clerical and administrative roles are most exposed globally, and that effects are strongly gendered.
Automation-dominant — high automation exposure, low augmentation offset
Augmentation-dominant — AI mostly assists rather than substitutes
Dual-exposed — both automation and augmentation apply; transition path uncertain
Low-exposed — limited AI applicability; stability but risk of exclusion from productivity gains
Strong normative grounding; covers structural fairness through gender/global dimensions. Less developed on working conditions and autonomy.
WEF four futures for jobs
2025
World Economic Forum · Future of Jobs Report 2025 / Four Futures 2030
Scenario-based macro framework projecting four possible job futures contingent on technology adoption and institutional response: Augmented humans (high tech + strong institutions), Efficiency machines (high tech + weak institutions), Human premium (moderate tech + strong institutions), Trapped (moderate tech + weak institutions). The key variable is not AI capability but governance and worker investment.
Augmented human — high-skill, human-AI complementarity, strong labour protections
Efficiency machine — AI-managed, productivity gains captured by capital, worker power eroded
Human premium — care, creativity, relational work valued; AI displaces routine
Trapped — exposure without investment; workers neither augmented nor transitioned
Best macro-level framing. Institutional variable is explicitly foregrounded. Less granular on within-occupation or task-level dynamics.
Synthesised archetypes

Eight patterns the literature identifies

Synthesising across the seven frameworks above, eight archetypes emerge — each with a distinct mechanism, a distinct evidence base, and a distinct fingerprint across the five Good Jobs dimensions. Where the literature disagrees about an archetype, that disagreement is noted.

01 · Literature consensus: strong
Task augmentation
AI handles sub-tasks; the human worker focuses on higher-order judgment. The job's skill ceiling rises faster than AI can follow. Found in professional, creative, and care roles with high human-need floors.
Mechanism (from literature)
Task decomposition: AI performs the bounded, rule-governed subtask while human retains the judgment-intensive remainder (Acemoglu & Restrepo task reinstatement; Brynjolfsson et al. recommender-augmented)
Productivity effect dominates displacement effect — total output rises and workers are retained (ILO augmentation-dominant pattern)
The human-need floor (regulatory, relational, accountability) prevents full substitution regardless of AI capability improvement (OpenAI Jobs Framework; ILO human need modifier)
Evidence base
Brynjolfsson et al. (2025): 15% productivity gain; customer sentiment improved; retention up — augmentation dominant
Eurofound EWCS (2024): ~40% of European workers report AI added tasks — the intensification risk is present even in augmentation archetypes
OECD AI-WIPS (2024): 4-in-5 workers say AI improved performance; 3-in-5 say enjoyment improved
Key disagreement: Pissarides Review / IFOW (2025) finds that many applications have reduced work quality as perceived by workers — augmentation is not guaranteed even with high AI capability
Acemoglu & Restrepo · Brynjolfsson et al. · ILO WP 96/140 · Eurofound EWCS · OECD AI-WIPS · IFOW Pissarides Review
Net opportunity — if job redesign deliberate
02 · Literature consensus: strong
Task displacement
AI substitutes for the human's task bundle substantially enough that demand for this worker category shrinks. The job may persist in name; the economic rationale for the worker does not. Acemoglu's "so-so automation" risk.
Mechanism (from literature)
Displacement effect dominates — AI performs most tasks at lower cost, reducing labour demand (Acemoglu & Restrepo 2018/2020; Frey & Osborne 2017)
"So-so automation": displacement occurs without sufficient reinstatement of new tasks to absorb displaced workers — net negative (Acemoglu 2024 simple macroeconomics of AI)
Low demand elasticity — productivity gains don't expand total demand enough to rehire displaced workers (ILO automation-dominant pattern; OpenAI low-elasticity classification)
Evidence base
Acemoglu & Restrepo (2020): one robot per 1,000 workers ⇒ −0.2pp employment, −0.42% wages in commuting zones
ILO (2025): global mean automation score 0.29; clerical roles most affected; ~1-in-4 workers in occupations with some GenAI exposure
Eloundou et al. (2024 Science): ~80% of US workers have ≥10% of tasks affected; ~19% have ≥50%
Key constraint: exposure ≠ displacement; ILO (2025) explicitly cautions against treating exposure measures as predictions
Acemoglu & Restrepo · Eloundou et al. · ILO WP 96/140 · Frey & Osborne · OpenAI Framework
Net risk — requires active transition support
03 · Literature consensus: strong
Algorithmic intensification
AI does not replace the worker but manages them — dictating pace, sequence, and evaluation. The task bundle may be unchanged but working conditions degrade through surveillance, reduced discretion, and performance pressure. The dominant pattern in low-wage service and platform work.
Mechanism (from literature)
Kellogg et al. (2020) 6 Rs — restricting, recording, and rating combine to create high-control environments without necessarily changing what workers do
Eurofound (2024): >40% of European workers report AI added tasks — intensification distinct from augmentation: pace rises, discretion falls
Fissured workplace amplifier: algorithmic management extends control to non-employees, enabling distance-management without employment obligations (Weil 2014; Schwartz)
Evidence base
Swedish logistics cross-sectional study (2024): algorithmic management associated with psychological distress (PR≈2.1), occupational accidents (PR≈1.9), MSK pain
Wood et al. (2019): platform work autonomy/control paradox — schedule flexibility coexists with tight algorithmic constraint
Equitable Growth (2024) US survey: automated management/surveillance exposure associated with negative health and wellbeing outcomes
arXiv 2512.22290: nonmonotonic effects — moderate algorithmic control may improve outcomes; very high control clearly harmful
Kellogg, Valentine & Christin · Wood et al. · Eurofound EWCS · Weil · Swedish logistics study · Equitable Growth
Net risk — strongest on work quality dimension
04 · Literature consensus: emerging
Expertise compression
AI transfers tacit expert knowledge to novice workers, compressing within-occupation skill premiums. Experts gain leverage (fewer do more); novices gain capability faster. The long-run risk is a hollowed pipeline — the expertise AI draws on may not be replenished if the learning pathway disappears.
Mechanism (from literature)
Brynjolfsson et al. (2025): AI externalises expert tacit knowledge through recommendations, enabling novices to perform at near-expert level — within-occupation wage compression follows
Deskilling paradox: AI scaffolds the tasks through which workers previously built foundational skills; short-run productivity gains may come at the cost of long-run skill pipeline (Rinta-Kahila et al.; Springer 2025 AI & Society)
Aviation analogue: autopilot deskilling of manual handling; Air France 447 cited in multiple deskilling papers as the failure mode when AI temporarily fails
Evidence base
Brynjolfsson et al. (2025): novice productivity gains ~30%; top-performer gains minimal or slightly negative — compresses premium
Springer (2025) "AI deskilling is a structural problem": erosion of activity awareness, competence maintenance, output assessment among heavy AI users
CIPD Good Work Index (2025): 16% of UK workers report AI-automated tasks; most report performance improvement but concerns about skill development cited
Key gap: long-run evidence on pipeline effects does not yet exist — this is the most speculative of the eight archetypes in evidential terms
Brynjolfsson et al. · Rinta-Kahila et al. · Springer AI & Society · CIPD Good Work Index · Vallor · Marin & Steinert
Dual-edged — short-run gain, long-run risk to pipeline
05 · Literature consensus: strong
Role reorganisation
The occupation persists but its internal structure changes substantially — some task clusters shrink or disappear, others expand or emerge. The worker retains employment but faces a significant relearning requirement. ILO's "dual-exposed" pattern; WEF's reorganisation scenario.
Mechanism (from literature)
ILO (2023/2025) dual-exposed pattern: both automation and augmentation apply to different task sub-clusters within the same occupation, producing net task composition change rather than displacement
WEF (2025): 39% of core skills expected to change by 2030 — implies role reorganisation for most professional occupations rather than full replacement
Acemoglu & Restrepo reinstatement effect: new tasks emerge within reorganised roles, but the timing and availability of retraining pathways determines whether workers capture this or are left behind
Evidence base
MOM Singapore (2026): 18.9% of firms that have adopted AI have redesigned job scopes; 13.9% created new AI-linked roles — consistent with reorganisation pattern dominating
WEF Future of Jobs (2025): 170M new jobs / 92M displaced net +78M; most employment change through reorganisation, not extinction
Eurofound (2024): 30% report tasks removed, >40% report tasks added — simultaneously: reorganisation in progress
Disagreement: Acemoglu (2024) estimates more modest effects than WEF/McKinsey — reorganisation scale uncertain
ILO WP 96/140 · Acemoglu & Restrepo · WEF Future of Jobs 2025 · MOM Singapore · Eurofound EWCS
Outcome uncertain — institutional investment determines direction
06 · Literature consensus: moderate
New occupation creation
AI generates entirely new occupational categories — roles whose primary purpose is to work with, on, or around AI systems. The evidence base is thin relative to the scale of disruption, and access to these roles is highly unequal.
Mechanism (from literature)
Acemoglu & Restrepo reinstatement effect — AI creates new task types that require new human capabilities; demand for AI-adjacent roles grows
WEF (2025): 170M new jobs projected by 2030, with AI and big data specialist and machine learning engineer among fastest-growing; but constrained by educational pipeline
Complementarity in high-skill work: demand for workers who can evaluate, supervise, and contextualise AI output grows faster than AI capability advances (MIT Work of the Future)
Evidence base
MOM Singapore (2026): 13.9% of firms adopting AI created new roles — still a minority; reinstatement effect present but modest at current diffusion stage
WEF (2025): technology-related roles comprise 6 of top 10 fastest-growing job categories by 2030
Key distributional concern: access to new AI-related occupations is concentrated in workers with existing high educational attainment — fails Structural Fairness dimension (IMF Khan 2024 on Singapore; CSIS on migrant worker exclusion)
Acemoglu (2024) dissent: macro productivity effects from AI may be more modest than optimists claim; reinstatement wave may be slower and smaller
Acemoglu & Restrepo · WEF Future of Jobs 2025 · MIT Work of the Future · MOM Singapore · IMF Khan 2024 · CSIS
Net opportunity — but access is structurally unequal
07 · Literature consensus: strong
Platform fissuring
AI amplifies the fissured workplace by extending algorithmic control to non-employee workers. The worker is neither employed nor protected: gig, platform, and contractor arrangements managed algorithmically at distance, combining AI's surveillance capabilities with the institutional voids of informal work.
Mechanism (from literature)
Weil (2014) fissuring + Schwartz "Watchful AI": AI enables lead firms to retain control without employment, expanding fissuring into new sectors and roles
Platform deactivation = the non-employment equivalent of dismissal — algorithmic, opaque, and largely uncontestable (Wood et al. 2019; Kellogg et al. 2020 replacing mechanism)
EPI (2023): unbalanced labor market power is what makes AI threatening — the fissuring archetype is the most extreme case of that imbalance
Evidence base
arXiv 2512.22290 algorithmic control and gig worker wellbeing: nonmonotonic — moderate algorithmic control neutral/positive; high control clearly harmful for performance AND wellbeing
Singapore Platform Workers Act (2024): CPF and injury protection extended to platform workers — but algorithmic transparency and contestation rights remain vague
CSIS (2024) on Singapore's 1.4M foreign workers: entirely outside CPF, SkillsFuture, PWM — the most extreme structural fairness failure
Strongest evidence base across all five dimensions — all negative, most acute for dimensions 4 and 5
Weil · Schwartz · Kellogg et al. · Wood et al. · EPI · arXiv 2512.22290 · Platform Workers Act SG · CSIS
Strongest risk archetype — all five dimensions degraded
08 · Literature consensus: emerging
Relational work under pressure
Care, teaching, counselling, and other relational occupations face a distinct challenge: AI can handle information and logistics tasks but the relational core — what makes these jobs meaningful and socially necessary — resists substitution. Yet cost pressures and productivity metrics applied to these roles can erode the relational core even without AI directly replacing it.
Mechanism (from literature)
Care work literature (Tronto; Held): relational work has irreducible human components (empathy, attunement, presence) that AI cannot replicate; but this does not protect workers from AI-enabled management pressures
Soldatic et al. (2026) on disability care: AI's relational meanings are contested — workers fear AI trivialises the emotional labour of care while deploying it as a productivity tool
Pissarides Review (2025): AI should be designed to augment relational workers, not replace the human relationship; but market incentives pull the other way
Evidence base
ILO (2023/2025): health professionals and education workers score high on augmentation-exposure index but also have strong human-need floors — dual-exposed but human-need-protected
arXiv patient-provider AI mediation study (2025): when AI mediates communication, relational quality changes in ways workers and recipients experience differently
Singapore demographics: 1-in-4 residents will be 65+ by 2030 — care workforce is the archetype with the most acute supply-demand pressure and most to lose from poor AI deployment
Key gap: most care work AI literature is qualitative; quantitative job-quality outcomes data for care workers under AI adoption is almost absent

What the literature misses

Critical gaps in the existing archetype literature

Identifying what existing frameworks do not cover is as important as synthesising what they do. These gaps define where original research — including this project — can make a genuine contribution.

Critical gap
Job quality outcomes under generative AI
The empirical literature almost entirely measures AI's effects on productivity, wages, and employment levels. What actually happens to autonomy, meaning, surveillance intensity, and development opportunities when AI is deployed in workplaces? Brynjolfsson et al. is the rare exception; Eurofound EWCS 2024 is beginning to fill the EU gap. Singapore has almost no primary data.
Critical gap
Singapore-specific peer-reviewed evidence
Singapore's AI-labour-market literature is almost entirely policy and grey literature (MOM, NTUC, IMF country analysis). Peer-reviewed academic work on how AI is actually transforming job quality for Singapore workers — especially lower-wage, migrant, and platform workers — is nearly absent. This is the most important gap for a Singapore-based project.
Critical gap
Long-run deskilling and pipeline effects
The expertise compression archetype raises a critical long-run concern: if AI handles the tasks through which workers build foundational competence, the expertise pipeline may hollow out even as short-run productivity rises. No quantitative longitudinal evidence exists yet. The aviation analogy suggests the risk is real; the magnitude is unknown.
Important gap
Fissuring + AI in non-Western contexts
The fissured workplace literature is almost entirely US-based. Singapore's high proportion of migrant workers on work permits — 1.4 million people entirely outside formal labour protections — represents an extreme version of fissuring that the existing literature does not model. The combination of algorithmic management and institutional exclusion is more acute here than anywhere the literature has studied.
Important gap
Care work and relational labour under AI
The relational work archetype has the weakest quantitative evidence base of the eight identified. Most care work AI literature is qualitative and ethical-framework-oriented. Given Singapore's demographic trajectory — acute care labour demand by 2030 — this gap is particularly consequential for this project.
Important gap
Operationalising labor market freedom
No existing framework operationalises "labor market freedom" — the ability to exit a bad job without catastrophic consequences — as a measurable, trackable dimension of job quality. Most frameworks subsume it under employment security or treat it as an institutional precondition. Building workable indicators for this dimension is a genuine methodological contribution this project could make.
Emerging gap
AI × monopsony and worker bargaining power
A newer literature (EPI 2023; Cheremukhin 2025; Dube et al.) argues that AI's threat to workers derives primarily from unbalanced labour market power, not technology itself. AI may amplify monopsony by reducing workers' outside options and information. This interaction has not yet been systematically integrated into job quality archetypes.
Emerging gap
Participatory AI design and archetype outcomes
Kochan et al. (MIT Sloan 2024) and IFOW–CIPD case studies suggest that worker voice in AI deployment systematically shifts outcomes toward better job quality. But the evidence is case-study level. Whether participatory design reliably shifts the archetype distribution — fewer intensification archetypes, more augmentation archetypes — has not been empirically tested at scale.
Emerging gap
Interaction between archetypes and demographic groups
The structural fairness dimension is almost entirely siloed from archetype analysis. The ILO identifies gender effects in exposure; CSIS identifies migrant worker risk. But which archetypes are most likely to affect which demographic groups — and why — is not systematically modelled. The intersection of archetype and structural position is a critical missing piece.
What this means

Three things the literature agrees on,
three things it doesn't

"The key variable is not AI capability but who controls deployment and what institutional context surrounds it."
Consensus 01
Institutions, not technology, determine which archetype dominates
Across MIT Work of the Future, ILO, OECD, IFOW, and WEF — the strongest consensus in the literature is that the same AI capability produces very different job quality outcomes depending on employer choices, worker voice, labour market regulation, and social protection. The WEF four-futures framework makes this explicit. Singapore's institutional context shapes which archetypes will dominate for its workers.
Consensus 02
Exposure ≠ displacement: most near-term change is task transformation, not job elimination
The ILO (2025) explicitly cautions against treating exposure measures as predictions of job loss. Eloundou et al. measure exposure; Brynjolfsson et al. study actual outcomes in deployment. The gap between "this task could be automated" and "this worker was made redundant" is filled by institutional decisions, demand elasticity, and the pace of AI diffusion. The reorganisation and intensification archetypes are empirically more common than displacement in current data.
Consensus 03
Algorithmic management is a distinct and underregulated job quality risk
Kellogg et al., Wood et al., the Swedish logistics study, and Equitable Growth all point to the same finding: being managed by an algorithm is consistently associated with worse health, psychological, and work quality outcomes, particularly when control is high and contestation rights are absent. This risk is present even when employment is preserved and wages are stable.
Economic security
Displacement and wage compression most studied; productivity gains present but distribution contested. Acemoglu pessimistic; Brynjolfsson cautiously optimistic; ILO focuses on task-transformation, not employment collapse.
Work quality
Best-evidenced dimension in qualitative terms. Algorithmic management literature is rich and consistent. Eurofound EWCS starting to provide quantitative backbone. Intensification the dominant pattern empirically; augmentation possible but not default.
Development
Expertise compression archetype supported by Brynjolfsson et al. and deskilling literature but long-run pipeline effects entirely unmeasured. This is the most important evidential gap for long-run job quality.
Labor market freedom
Least-developed in the literature. Monopsony and platform fissuring literatures address it implicitly. No existing framework operationalises it as a measurable dimension. This is the most distinctive contribution of the Good Jobs Framework relative to OECD/ILO/Eurofound.
Structural fairness
ILO identifies gender effects; CSIS and EPI identify migrant worker and low-wage worker concentration of risks. But intersection of archetype and structural position is not modelled. The most important gap for Singapore specifically.
Where existing archetypes are incomplete
The existing frameworks collectively identify the eight archetypes above. What they do not do is: (1) provide quantitative job-quality outcome data for most archetypes; (2) model how archetypes interact with structural demographic position; (3) connect archetype to policy lever in actionable ways; or (4) account for Singapore's specific institutional context. These four gaps are where this project's original contribution sits.

Five Dimensions

AI through the lens of job quality

Each dimension of a good job has distinct aspects — sub-components that AI affects in different ways and to different degrees. The goal is not to ask whether AI is "good or bad for work" but to identify precisely where the risks concentrate and where the genuine opportunities lie.

1
Dimension 01
Economic Security
What the job provides while you are in it — wages, income stability, benefits, and protection from arbitrary dismissal.

Economic security is the dimension where AI's effects are most visible and most debated. The displacement risk is real — AI can and does substitute for tasks, reducing headcount and putting downward pressure on wages in exposed occupations. But the picture is more complicated than simple displacement: AI also raises productivity in ways that, with the right institutions, can raise wages and reduce economic volatility.

The key variable is who captures the productivity gains. Without deliberate policy, firms capture most of the surplus while workers bear most of the transition risk. With the right mechanisms — sector wage agreements, profit-sharing, progressive wage models tied to AI adoption — the gains can be distributed more fairly.

Wages and income level
Whether pay is adequate relative to the cost of living and fairly reflects the value produced
Risks
AI substitution reduces labour demand in exposed tasks, putting downward pressure on wages for routine and semi-routine workers
Productivity gains from AI adoption accrue disproportionately to capital, compressing the labour share of income
Gig platforms use AI to commoditise labour, making it harder for workers to bargain for higher pay
Opportunities
AI raises productivity, creating a surplus that — with wage agreements or profit-sharing — can fund higher wages
AI tools can help workers understand their market value and identify better-paid alternatives, improving bargaining position
Progressive Wage Models tied to AI adoption can mandate that productivity gains translate to worker pay
Net balance
Tilts toward risk without policy intervention
Income stability and predictability
Whether income is reliable enough to plan a life — consistent hours, no sudden changes, income during illness
Risks
AI scheduling systems optimise for firm efficiency, creating volatile and unpredictable hours for workers — especially in retail, hospitality, and logistics
Platform AI algorithms change pay rates and order availability without notice, making income planning impossible
AI-driven performance management can trigger sudden role changes or dismissal with limited notice
Opportunities
AI demand forecasting can smooth hiring patterns, reducing seasonal volatility for workers if firms choose to share that predictability
AI can identify workers at risk of income disruption earlier, enabling proactive support rather than crisis response
Better income data from AI systems could make wage insurance and automatic stabilisers more precisely targeted
Net balance
Significant risk without scheduling transparency rules
Employment protection
Protection against arbitrary dismissal — adequate notice, fair process, and recourse if wrongly let go
Risks
AI-based performance monitoring enables dismissal decisions based on opaque algorithmic scores, making it harder for workers to understand or contest outcomes
Automated retrenchment decisions — flagging workers whose tasks are automatable — may not follow fair process requirements
Platform deactivation (the gig worker equivalent of dismissal) is already largely algorithmic and largely uncontestable
Opportunities
AI can flag early when a role is at risk, giving workers more lead time to retrain rather than facing sudden redundancy
Algorithmic transparency requirements — mandating that AI dismissal recommendations be explainable — could improve procedural fairness
AI tools for workers to understand and document their own performance provide better grounds for contesting unfair dismissal
Net balance
Risk dominant; transparency regulation could shift balance
Non-wage benefits
Healthcare, pension contributions, leave entitlements — the full package of security beyond the wage
Risks
AI accelerates the shift to gig and contract work, where benefit coverage is typically absent or minimal
Firms using AI to reduce headcount often cut the most benefit-rich roles and retain leaner, less protected ones
Workers in AI-displaced roles transitioning to lower-tier employment often lose benefit levels they had accumulated
Opportunities
Singapore's Platform Workers Act extension (CPF contributions for gig workers) partially addresses this for platform workers
AI productivity gains provide fiscal space to fund portable benefit systems decoupled from employment status
AI-powered benefits navigation tools can help workers understand and access entitlements they currently miss
Net balance
Moderate risk; policy trajectory matters enormously
2
Dimension 02
Work Quality
How the work feels — autonomy, meaning, freedom from excessive surveillance, and time for life outside work.

Work quality is the dimension where AI's effects are most direct and most varied across different types of work. For some workers, AI is genuinely augmenting — removing tedious tasks and freeing attention for higher-order judgment and creative work. For others, particularly in lower-wage service and logistics roles, AI is primarily a surveillance and control technology, intensifying monitoring and reducing the discretion workers can exercise.

The crucial distinction is between AI deployed to serve the worker and AI deployed to manage the worker. Both happen under the same label. Which one predominates depends on who controls the technology — and who has a say in its deployment.

Task autonomy and discretion
Whether workers can make meaningful decisions about how they do their work, not just whether they do it
Risks
AI systems that provide step-by-step instructions or constrain how tasks are performed reduce worker discretion, even when not replacing the worker entirely
Algorithmic management in logistics, retail, and platform work dictates pace, sequence, and method — leaving little room for worker judgment
Deskilling: as AI handles the complex parts of a job, workers may be left with execution-only roles that require less judgment over time
Opportunities
AI can handle routine and administrative tasks, freeing workers to focus on the judgment-intensive, relational, and creative aspects of their role
AI decision-support tools — which present options rather than directives — can enhance rather than replace human judgment
In professional roles, AI can take over compliance and documentation work, expanding the space for professional discretion
Net balance
Highly variable — depends on deployment design
Meaning and purpose
Whether the work feels worthwhile — contributing to something beyond a paycheck, using real skills, mattering to others
Risks
When AI takes over the skilled core of a profession — the part that made the work feel meaningful — workers may retain the role without the meaning (e.g. doctors reviewing AI diagnoses rather than diagnosing)
Professional identity erosion: workers whose expertise is being automated may experience a loss of the sense of competence and mastery that gave work its value
Reduced sense of contribution when AI output is hard to distinguish from worker output
Opportunities
By absorbing routine and administrative tasks, AI can concentrate work on the aspects most workers find meaningful — judgment, care, creativity, relationship
In care and education, AI can reduce the burden of documentation and monitoring, allowing workers to focus on the human interactions that motivated them into the field
AI can make the impact of work more visible — showing care workers patient outcomes, teachers learning trajectories — increasing sense of contribution
Net balance
Sector-dependent; job redesign determines outcome
Surveillance and monitoring intensity
The degree to which workers are watched, measured, and evaluated — and whether that monitoring feels intrusive or fair
Risks
AI dramatically lowers the cost of intensive monitoring — keystroke logging, productivity scoring, location tracking, sentiment analysis — making surveillance that was previously impractical now routine
Monitoring creates stress, reduces intrinsic motivation, and signals to workers that they are not trusted — degrading the employment relationship even when it doesn't lead to direct action
Workers typically cannot see what is being collected about them or how it is used, creating power asymmetry that is experienced as a form of coercion
Opportunities
AI monitoring can be used to protect workers rather than manage them — detecting unsafe working conditions, identifying bullying patterns, flagging excessive workloads
Transparent, worker-accessible monitoring data can give workers better evidence for performance reviews and workload disputes
Algorithmic transparency requirements could create a right to explanation — workers understanding how monitoring data is used in decisions about them
Net balance
Strong risk; one of AI's clearest negative effects on work quality
Working hours and rest
Whether work hours are reasonable, predictable, and leave enough time for life — recovery, family, community, self
Risks
AI blurs the boundaries of work — always-on communication tools, AI assistants available at any hour, and remote work normalisation can extend work into all available time
AI scheduling optimisation in retail and hospitality creates volatile shift patterns that make it difficult to plan non-work life
Productivity expectations rise as AI makes workers more efficient — the time saved may be absorbed by additional work rather than returned as rest
Opportunities
Genuine AI productivity gains could — with collective agreements or regulatory mandates — reduce required working hours while maintaining output
AI scheduling tools, designed with worker preferences as a constraint rather than an afterthought, can improve schedule predictability
AI can handle asynchronous workloads that currently require workers to be available outside hours — reducing the need for real-time responsiveness
Net balance
Risk without explicit working time protections
3
Dimension 03
Development
Career progression pathways, skills growth, and the genuine accumulation of capabilities that expand future options.

Development is the dimension most shaped by the deskilling paradox: AI can free workers from routine tasks, but those routine tasks are often exactly how workers build foundational competence. The junior lawyer who can no longer practice drafting because AI does it may never develop the judgment to supervise the AI well. The question is whether AI is removing the scaffold before the building is up.

At the same time, AI creates genuinely new skill demands — in prompting, evaluation, AI tool management, and human-AI collaboration — that, if workers can access training in them, represent real development opportunities. The distribution of that access is the central development equity question.

Skill acquisition and deepening
Whether the job actively builds capabilities — through learning by doing, challenge, mentorship, and practice
Risks
When AI handles the tasks through which workers previously built skills, the on-the-job learning pathway disappears — workers are present in the job but not developing within it
Junior and entry-level roles are often most automated first, eliminating the apprenticeship structure that has historically built professional competence
Over-reliance on AI tools can reduce tolerance for the productive struggle and error that is necessary for deep skill development
Opportunities
AI tutors and on-demand learning systems can provide personalised skill development, allowing workers to learn what they need when they need it
By handling lower-level tasks, AI can create space for workers to engage more regularly with complex problems that build higher-order skills
AI simulation environments allow workers to practice skills and make mistakes in low-stakes contexts — accelerating skill development in some fields
Net balance
Risk real, especially for entry-level workers
Career pathway clarity
Whether there is a legible, realistic route from where you are to somewhere better — in terms of skill, responsibility, and pay
Risks
AI disrupts established career ladders — the pathway from junior analyst to senior analyst to manager may no longer be viable if AI handles the junior work
In reorganised occupations, it is genuinely unclear what the career pathway looks like — the destination jobs do not yet exist in their final form
Workers in declining occupations face a career transition requirement but lack clear guidance on what credible pathways look like
Opportunities
AI-powered career guidance tools can make labour market data legible at the individual level — showing realistic transition pathways based on actual skill overlap and demand signals
Singapore's PWM provides a model for making career pathways explicit and binding — AI adoption requirements within PWM could mandate new pathway design
AI creates new occupational categories with genuine career depth — AI engineers, AI governance specialists, human-AI collaboration roles
Net balance
Pathway disruption is acute; intelligence tools partially address it
Access to training and retraining
Whether workers have real access — in time, money, and fit — to training that keeps their skills current and opens new pathways
Risks
The speed of AI capability development means training provision will perpetually lag — courses accredited today may be obsolete before workers complete them
Workers in the most at-risk occupations — lower-wage, older, less educated — have the lowest training uptake, precisely when they need it most
Employer-provided AI training is concentrated in high-skill professional roles, leaving lower-wage workers to self-fund and self-navigate
Opportunities
AI-powered personalised learning can significantly reduce the time and cost of retraining, making shorter, more targeted transitions feasible
AI tools can identify individual skill gaps precisely, allowing training investment to be targeted rather than generic
Singapore's SkillsFuture infrastructure is well-positioned to be updated with AI-specific content at scale, if course design keeps pace with AI development
Net balance
Mixed — access gaps remain structural; AI can help but won't self-solve
Wage growth over career
Whether accumulated experience and skill translate into rising pay — not just at entry but throughout the working life
Risks
AI compresses the wage returns to experience in occupations where AI substitutes for the judgment that experience builds — experienced workers' wage premium erodes
Older workers displaced mid-career face retraining requirements at an age when employer investment in training is lowest
In reorganised occupations, accumulated expertise in the old version of the job may not transfer — workers effectively restart at entry-level pay
Opportunities
AI creates new premium skill categories — AI tool expertise, human-AI workflow design — where the wage premium for competence is currently very high
Productivity gains from AI can fund wage growth for workers who remain and upskill, if PWM-style mechanisms mandate it
AI-powered mentorship tools can help older workers transfer their contextual expertise into new AI-augmented roles, preserving some wage premium
Net balance
Mid-career workers face acute risk; entry-level opportunities offset this partially
4
Dimension 04
Labor Market Freedom
The power to say no — the degree to which a worker can exit a bad job without catastrophic consequences.

Labor market freedom is the dimension most directly shaped by AI's macroeconomic effects. As AI displaces occupations and reduces the number of viable alternatives in certain labour market segments, the cost of leaving a bad employer rises. Workers in at-risk occupations have less credible exit threats, which weakens their position even within jobs that haven't yet been automated.

Paradoxically, this is also the dimension where AI's productivity gains offer the most transformative opportunity: if AI-generated surplus funds universal basic services — affordable housing, portable healthcare, strong unemployment insurance — the coercive power of any individual employer over any individual worker diminishes fundamentally. That is the post-scarcity possibility. Getting there requires deliberate redistribution of the surplus.

Quality and coverage of outside options
Whether there are genuinely good alternative jobs available — not just any job, but jobs that are as good or better than the current one
Risks
AI reduces the number of viable alternative roles in exposed occupation clusters, narrowing the realistic exit options for workers in those segments
Transition to available alternatives often involves significant quality degradation — moving from a stable PMET role to a gig or lower-skilled role to survive
Geographic concentration of AI-safe roles in high-skill sectors means outside options are not equally distributed across the workforce
Opportunities
AI creates new occupation categories — AI trainers, human-AI workflow designers, AI governance roles — that expand the option set for some workers
AI-powered job matching and career transition tools can make existing good alternatives more visible and accessible
Expanding care, education, and community services through direct public investment creates quality job options in sectors with genuine demand growth
Net balance
Risk for exposed workers; opportunity concentrated in high-skill segment
Social protection during transitions
Whether the welfare system adequately supports workers between jobs — income replacement, healthcare, retraining support
Risks
Singapore's CPF-based social protection is tied to formal employment — workers in gig, contract, or self-employed AI-adjacent roles may contribute less and receive less protection
If AI disruption accelerates faster than policy adaptation, the gap between when workers need support and when it becomes available could widen
Means-tested support systems are poorly designed for the intermittent or graduated income reduction that AI-driven transitions often produce
Opportunities
AI productivity gains generate fiscal surplus that can fund more generous and universal social protection without requiring higher tax rates on labour
AI systems can identify workers approaching transition risk earlier, enabling proactive support outreach before crisis rather than after
Digital delivery of social protection — AI-assisted eligibility checking, automated claim processing — can significantly reduce the friction of accessing support
Net balance
Genuine opportunity if surplus is redistributed; risk if it is not
Portability of benefits and credentials
Whether what you've built — savings, healthcare coverage, professional credentials — travels with you when you change jobs
Risks
Professional credentials lose value faster as AI changes what competencies are needed — workers who have invested in certifications may find them depreciated before they've recovered the cost
Employer-specific training and AI tool familiarity may not transfer to new employers who use different systems, reducing effective portability of built skills
CPF housing lock-in — where housing affordability depends on CPF balances — reduces geographic and occupational mobility in ways that constrain exit power
Opportunities
AI-powered skills assessment and verification could replace or supplement credential systems with more dynamic, task-level competency records that transfer across employers
Portable benefit architectures — individual learning accounts, sector-wide pension pools — are administratively more feasible with AI systems managing the complexity
AI can help workers identify which of their skills are genuinely transferable versus employer-specific, enabling better transition planning
Net balance
Risk predominates; credential reform and portable systems offer real opportunity
Universal basic services adequacy
Whether housing, healthcare, education, and care are affordable enough that no worker is forced to stay in a bad job just to survive
Risks
If AI-driven productivity gains are captured by capital rather than redistributed, the cost of basic services may rise relative to wages — increasing rather than decreasing the coercive power of employment
Housing affordability in Singapore constrains exit power in ways that are deeply structural — HDB asset values create CPF dependencies that make leaving formal employment costly
Care costs — childcare, eldercare — may rise as AI displaces lower-wage care workers, perversely increasing the cost of the safety net that would otherwise enable exit
Opportunities
AI productivity gains in housing, healthcare, and education delivery could reduce the cost of providing these services — making universal provision fiscally viable at higher quality
This is the post-scarcity opportunity: if AI-generated surplus funds a genuine universal basic services floor, the coercive power of any individual employer over any individual worker diminishes structurally
Singapore's existing UBS infrastructure (HDB, polyclinics, public education) provides a foundation — AI could extend reach and quality without proportionally increasing cost
Net balance
Genuine opportunity — highest leverage dimension for long-term non-coercion
5
Dimension 05
Structural Fairness
Whether good jobs are equally accessible — whether AI's risks and opportunities are distributed fairly across class, gender, race, and status.

Structural fairness is not a separate dimension from the others — it is a lens applied across all of them. The question is not whether AI affects job quality, but whether it affects it equally. Every risk identified in dimensions 1–4 is unevenly distributed. Every opportunity is unevenly accessible. Understanding who bears which risk and who captures which opportunity is inseparable from understanding AI's impact on work.

Singapore's specific context adds layers of complexity: a multicultural workforce with documented disparities across racial lines; a massive migrant worker population entirely outside formal protection; a gender dimension shaped by caregiving norms and occupational segregation; and a class dimension defined by the PMET/non-PMET divide that shapes access to essentially everything.

Differential AI exposure by class
Whether AI's risks are concentrated among lower-wage workers while its opportunities accrue to higher-wage workers
Risks
Automation risk is highest in routine, task-predictable work concentrated in lower-wage occupations — cleaning, data entry, customer service, logistics — while higher-wage professional roles are more likely to be augmented than automated
Lower-wage workers have less access to retraining, less savings buffer during transitions, and less social capital to navigate the new labour market
AI productivity gains in sectors employing lower-wage workers may result in headcount reduction rather than wage gains, concentrating the downside
Opportunities
AI tutoring and upskilling tools are potentially more equalising than classroom education — quality learning becomes accessible without expensive tuition
AI career navigation tools can give lower-wage workers access to labour market intelligence that previously required expensive career coaching
Progressive Wage Models explicitly designed for AI-adoption contexts can mandate that productivity gains in sectors employing lower-wage workers are shared
Net balance
Strongly risk-skewed without deliberate redistribution policy
Gender dimensions of AI transition
Whether AI's effects on work quality and security are experienced differently by women — through occupational segregation, caregiving, and bias
Risks
Occupations with high female concentration — administrative, clerical, customer service — have above-average AI exposure, placing women disproportionately in the automation risk category
AI hiring and promotion tools trained on historical data encode historical gender bias — potentially reproducing patterns of under-promotion and pay gaps at scale
Care work — disproportionately done by women, paid and unpaid — is at risk of both automation (reducing care jobs) and underinvestment (AI replacing human care in ways that reduce care quality)
Opportunities
AI tools for blind CV screening and structured interviews can reduce the influence of gender bias in hiring decisions when deliberately designed for this purpose
Flexible AI-enabled work arrangements could benefit women who carry disproportionate caregiving responsibilities — if organisations choose to enable rather than surveil remote work
AI productivity gains in care delivery could reduce the physical burden of care work, making it more sustainable as a long-term career
Net balance
Risk predominates; bias in AI systems is a compounding factor
Race and ethnicity in AI labour outcomes
Whether AI adoption amplifies or reduces racial and ethnic disparities in job quality, access, and wages
Risks
In Singapore, Malay workers are overrepresented in lower-wage, higher-automation-risk occupations — meaning AI exposure risk is unevenly distributed by ethnicity
AI systems trained predominantly on English-language data perform worse for non-English speakers, creating access barriers in multilingual Singapore
AI hiring tools may encode ethnic bias from historical hiring patterns, particularly given documented labour market disparities in Singapore
Opportunities
AI tools deliberately designed for multilingual contexts — Singapore's four official languages plus dialects — could improve access for non-English-dominant communities
Structured AI hiring processes, if audited for bias, could reduce the influence of informal networks and referrals that currently disadvantage minority applicants
Mendaki and community-based organisations can use AI tools to target retraining and navigation support at communities with highest transition need
Net balance
Risk without deliberate bias auditing and multilingual AI design
Migrant worker exclusion
Whether the 1.4 million migrant workers in Singapore are included in or further excluded from AI transition protections and opportunities
Risks
Migrant workers in construction, domestic work, and lower-wage services are largely outside CPF, SkillsFuture, PWM, and every other transition support mechanism — they bear AI transition risk with no institutional buffer
AI-driven productivity in sectors employing migrant workers may accelerate their replacement without any corresponding transition support or wage benefit
Algorithmic management of migrant workers — in domestic placement platforms, construction scheduling systems — is subject to even less accountability than for citizens
Opportunities
AI tools providing migrant workers with information about their rights, working conditions, and available support in their own languages could meaningfully improve their position
Platform-based worker co-ops using AI for scheduling and matching — designed with worker voice — could provide fairer alternatives to employer-controlled platforms
AI monitoring of migrant worker conditions by advocacy organisations (TWC2, HOME) could create accountability pressure that formal systems do not
Net balance
Strongest risk concentration in the framework — structural exclusion is the baseline
Synthesis

Where the risks concentrate,
where the opportunities lie

Across the five dimensions and twenty aspects, a consistent pattern emerges: AI's default trajectory — without deliberate policy — tilts toward eroding job quality. The opportunities are real but they require active choices to realise.

01
Economic Security
Displacement and wage compression are real. Productivity surplus can fund better wages — but only with policy that mandates distribution.
02
Work Quality
Surveillance and autonomy erosion are the clearest negatives. Augmentation and meaning enhancement are real but unevenly distributed.
03
Development
Deskilling and pathway disruption are acute especially for junior and mid-career workers. AI learning tools partially offset this.
04
LM Freedom
The highest-opportunity dimension. AI surplus funding universal basic services is the most powerful long-term lever for non-coercion.
05
Structural Fairness
Every risk is unequally distributed. Migrant workers, lower-wage workers, and women bear the most while having the least recourse.

The overarching finding: AI does not have a fixed effect on job quality. It sharpens the power of whoever controls deployment. Where workers, unions, and governments have agency over how AI is introduced and what happens to its gains — job quality can improve. Where that agency is absent, the default is extraction.

What this means

Implications by audience

The AI risk and opportunity map has different implications depending on who you are and what levers you have.

For workers and unions
Know which risks are coming and demand a voice in how AI arrives
The risks to autonomy and surveillance are the most immediate — push for algorithmic transparency and contestation rights before deployment, not after
Collective bargaining agreements need AI provisions: notice requirements before technology changes, worker participation in job redesign, limits on monitoring intensity
The labor market freedom dimension is the long game — advocate for portable benefits, stronger unemployment support, and universal basic services as the structural conditions for non-coercion
Migrant and gig workers face the most concentrated risk with the least protection — any worker movement that doesn't address their situation is incomplete
For employers and policymakers
AI's productivity gains come with a design choice about who benefits
Job redesign that captures AI gains as efficiency savings without worker participation is likely to degrade work quality and increase turnover — undermining the productivity gains themselves
The WDG(JR+) grant methodology should require worker participation in redesign as a condition — this shifts the balance toward augmentation rather than pure substitution
Algorithmic transparency requirements for HR AI — hiring, performance management, scheduling — are low-cost regulatory interventions with high job quality returns
Investing AI productivity gains in universal basic services is both the most equitable redistribution and the most effective way to create a labour market where workers genuinely choose their jobs
For researchers and educators
The measurement gap is itself the most important finding
Singapore has no longitudinal working conditions survey — meaning the job quality impacts of AI adoption are currently invisible in official data. Advocating for this measurement infrastructure is a priority contribution
Curriculum design for an AI era should prepare students not just to use AI tools but to understand their rights and agency in AI-transformed workplaces
Research on AI and job quality needs to centre the experiences of workers who are most exposed and least heard — migrant workers, ITE graduates, older displaced workers — rather than the PMETs who are easier to reach
The participatory design process is itself a data collection opportunity — worker deliberation about what they value in work produces richer quality data than any survey instrument alone
Transformation Archetypes

Six ways AI
transforms a job

The twenty aspects map the territory. But AI doesn't affect jobs as a checklist — it transforms them through recognisable patterns. Six archetypes describe the most common trajectories, each with a distinct signature across the Good Jobs dimensions.

Archetype 01
The Hollowed Expert
AI handles the skilled core of the role. The worker remains — as reviewer, validator, or overseer — but the expertise that justified the wage premium and gave the work meaning is progressively absorbed by the system.
Dimension signature
Dim 1: Wages compress as expertise is commoditised
Dim 2: Meaning erodes as AI absorbs the skilled core
Dim 3: Junior pipeline collapses — no path to senior expertise
Dim 2: Freed from routine work — if redesigned around remaining human judgment
The defining risk: workers retain the title and the accountability but not the competence or the pay. They become liability-holders for AI systems they didn't design and can't fully evaluate.
Concrete examples
Radiologist, Singapore public hospital
AI reads scans with comparable accuracy. Radiologist reviews flagged cases and signs off. Reading volume stays the same or rises; the diagnostic judgment that defined the profession — and justified 12 years of training — is narrowed to edge cases.
Junior lawyer, Singapore law firm
AI handles contract review, due diligence, and first-draft pleadings. The junior role that previously built advocacy and drafting skill through practice no longer provides that scaffold — and fewer junior positions are needed.
Financial analyst, MNC Singapore office
AI generates market reports, earnings models, and scenario analyses. The analyst curates, contextualises, and presents. The value-added is increasingly editorial rather than analytical — but the role is still labelled analyst.
Archetype 02
The Augmented Craftsperson
AI absorbs the administrative, repetitive, or compliance-driven parts of a job, freeing the worker to concentrate on the highest-value, most judgment-intensive, most creative, or most relational aspects of their craft. The job genuinely improves.
Dimension signature
Dim 2: Autonomy and meaning both rise as AI handles what workers found burdensome
Dim 3: Development accelerates — more time for complex problems
Dim 1: Productivity gains can support wage growth if institutions mandate it
Dim 1: Risk that productivity gains are absorbed by higher output expectations
The defining condition: this archetype only materialises when job redesign is deliberate. Without active redesign, freed time is simply absorbed into higher volume expectations — producing a Managed Body instead.
Concrete examples
Secondary school teacher, Singapore MOE school
AI handles marking, lesson plan drafting, and parent communication drafts. Teacher spends reclaimed time on individual student conversations, project-based learning facilitation, and mentoring. The relational core of teaching — which teachers say motivates them — expands.
Surgeon, Singapore private hospital
AI pre-operative planning, instrument guidance, and post-operative monitoring reduce the cognitive load on routine aspects of procedures. The surgeon's judgment — on complex cases, on patient communication, on intra-operative adaptation — becomes more central, not less.
Social worker, MSF Community Centre
AI handles case documentation, eligibility checking across multiple schemes, and appointment scheduling. Social worker redirects saved hours toward home visits and sustained relationship-building with clients — the work they entered the profession to do.
Archetype 03
The Managed Body
The worker is necessary — their physical presence, strength, or service interaction cannot be automated — but AI is deployed primarily to surveil, pace, and control them. The job is not eliminated but its quality is systematically degraded.
Dimension signature
Dim 2: Severe — autonomy near-zero, monitoring intensive, meaning eroded
Dim 1: Income volatile, hours algorithmically determined
Dim 3: No development pathway — productivity pressure crowds out learning
Dim 4: Low exit power — skills not transferable, few comparable alternatives
The defining feature: AI is management's tool, deployed against the worker's interests. The same scheduling and monitoring technology could be designed to serve workers — but without worker voice in procurement, it typically isn't.
Concrete examples
Food delivery rider, Grab/Foodpanda Singapore
Route optimisation, surge pricing, order batching, and acceptance rate monitoring are all AI-managed. The rider's income, hours, and workload are determined by an algorithm they cannot see or contest. Post-Platform Workers Act, CPF is now mandatory — but algorithmic governance remains opaque.
Cleaning supervisor, large commercial building
GPS-tracked cleaning rounds, sensor-verified task completion, and AI-generated performance scores have intensified monitoring without giving supervisors any new tools or discretion. The score determines contract renewal prospects — but workers cannot access or query it.
Customer service agent, large bank call centre
Real-time AI sentiment scoring of calls, automated handle-time monitoring, and AI-suggested responses that agents are expected to use. The agent's judgment is constrained to a small decision space; their emotional performance is continuously measured and fed back.
Archetype 04
The Displaced Generalist
AI can handle most of the tasks that justified the position. The role is not just changed but eliminated or so severely compressed that it effectively disappears. The worker's skills are real — but no longer sufficiently differentiated from what AI can do reliably and cheaply.
Dimension signature
Dim 1: Income and employment security both collapse
Dim 3: Career pathway effectively ends — no natural next role
Dim 4: Exit options limited — comparable roles shrinking across the economy
Dim 3: Transferable skills may map to growth roles — if transition support exists
The defining policy question: does transition support arrive before displacement rather than after? Workers in this archetype are typically visible in data only once their income has already collapsed.
Concrete examples
Data entry and processing clerk
The canonical case. Document processing, form handling, data migration, and invoice matching are all within reliable AI capability at lower cost. Headcount has been reducing steadily without individual role eliminations — through attrition rather than layoffs, making the trend invisible until it's complete.
Mid-level HR generalist
AI handles job description writing, CV screening, interview scheduling, onboarding document generation, and policy query answering. The generalist role — which previously required broad knowledge across all these areas — no longer justifies a full-time position in many mid-size firms.
Travel and insurance agent
Comparison, booking, and basic advisory functions are reliably AI-handled at near-zero marginal cost. The residual human value — complex problem-solving, high-stakes advice, relationship management — supports a much smaller number of higher-skill agents rather than the previous generalist population.
Archetype 05
The New Specialist
Roles that either didn't exist before AI or have been dramatically elevated by its arrival. Skill demand is high, wages reflect genuine scarcity, and the work involves deep engagement with AI systems themselves — not just alongside them.
Dimension signature
Dim 1: High wages, strong demand, skills premium currently very high
Dim 2: High autonomy, intellectually engaging, rapidly evolving
Dim 3: Rapid skill accumulation; new credentials forming in real time
Dim 5: Access heavily skewed to already-privileged; deep fairness concern
The defining equity tension: these roles are the good news in the AI jobs picture — but they are overwhelmingly accessible only to workers who already had educational and social capital advantages. They do not naturally absorb displaced generalists.
Concrete examples
AI governance officer, financial institution
Evaluates AI systems for regulatory compliance, fairness, and model risk. Requires deep knowledge of both the technical architecture and the regulatory landscape. Role barely existed five years ago; MAS regulation has made it mandatory for all significant AI deployments in finance.
Human-AI workflow designer, management consultancy
Designs the division of labour between human and AI in client delivery processes — which decisions stay with humans, which handoffs are automated, where human judgment adds irreplaceable value. Blends organisational design with technical AI literacy.
AI trainer / RLHF specialist
Provides domain-expert feedback to improve AI system outputs — a role that requires genuine professional expertise (in law, medicine, finance, etc.) combined with the ability to articulate judgment in ways that improve model training. Niche, well-paid, and currently very short supply.
Archetype 06
The Care Anchor
Roles where the human relationship is the irreducible core — where what is being provided is not a service but a presence, and that presence cannot be automated without destroying the thing being provided. AI changes the context but not the essence of the work.
Dimension signature
Dim 2: Meaning preserved — relational core is irreducibly human
Dim 1 & 3: AI can free time for higher-quality care and skill development
Dim 2: Risk that AI monitoring of care interactions degrades the relationship
Dim 1: Historically undervalued — AI productivity argument could fund wage rises
The defining opportunity: Singapore's aging demographics mean these roles are growing in number. If AI handles administrative burden and AI productivity funds wage rises, care work could become one of the best archetypes in the post-AI labour market — but only with deliberate policy.
Concrete examples
Eldercare worker, Singapore nursing home
AI handles medication reminders, fall detection, vital monitoring, and scheduling. The care worker's time is redirected toward the irreplaceable — conversation, touch, emotional presence, dignity. Singapore's 1-in-4-over-65 demographic by 2030 makes this the fastest-growing archetype in the economy.
School counsellor, MOE secondary school
AI identifies students showing early stress or academic risk signals, enabling the counsellor to prioritise proactively. The counselling relationship itself — trust, confidentiality, empathy, sustained presence — remains entirely human. AI extends reach without substituting for the core.
Community development officer, People's Association
AI supports programme design, resident needs analysis, and event logistics. The officer's role — building trust with residents, navigating community politics, representing diverse voices in planning — is irreducibly relational and contextual. AI makes the operational work faster; the human work remains human.
A note on movement between archetypes
Most workers will not stay in one archetype throughout their career. The same occupation may shift archetype as AI capability develops — a role that begins as an Augmented Craftsperson may become a Hollowed Expert as AI encroaches further into the skilled core, or a Displaced Generalist as capabilities reach a threshold. Understanding which direction an occupation is moving — and how fast — is as important as understanding where it is today.
The Good Jobs Framework's five dimensions apply differently to each archetype. For a Managed Body worker, Dimension 2 (surveillance) is the most urgent intervention point. For a Hollowed Expert, Dimension 3 (development pathways) and the restructuring of professional identity are central. For a Care Anchor, the priority is ensuring that AI productivity gains translate into Dimension 1 (wage) improvements rather than being absorbed by the employing institution. Archetype determines which levers to pull.
